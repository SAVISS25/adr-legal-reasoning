**PAPER METADATA**

*   **Full citation:** Robert H. Mnookin & Lewis Kornhauser, _Bargaining in the Shadow of the Law: The Case of Divorce_, **88 Yale L.J. 950** (1979).
*   **Academic tradition / school of thought:** Law-and-economics–inflected bargaining theory and “private ordering” within the Legal Process tradition (explicitly invoking Hart & Sacks; conceptually aligned with Fuller/Eisenberg-style private ordering through negotiation).
*   **Core field:** **negotiation** (with strong overlap: ADR theory / interdisciplinary)

**CENTRAL THESIS (one paragraph)**

The paper argues that the “primary function of contemporary divorce law” is “not as imposing order from above, but rather as providing a framework within which divorcing couples can themselves determine their postdissolution rights and responsibilities,” i.e., a regime of **private ordering** in which “the rules and procedures used in court for adjudicating disputes affect the bargaining process that occurs between divorcing couples outside the courtroom”; accordingly, to understand (and reform) divorce law one must analyze how legal entitlements, uncertainty, transaction costs, and strategic behavior shape negotiated settlements “in the shadow of the law,” not merely how courts decide litigated cases.

**THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK**

*   **Framework name (as used in the paper):** **“Bargaining in the shadow of the law”** + **private ordering** + a **simple bargaining model** of divorce negotiation.
*   **Intellectual lineage (explicit + implicit):**
    *   **Legal Process / institutionalism:** The paper quotes Hart & Sacks on how societies “assign\[\] many kinds of questions to private decision” and “back\[\] up the private decision” when later challenged, framing private ordering as institutionally central.
    *   **Private ordering through negotiation:** It cites (and conceptually tracks) Melvin Eisenberg’s account of “Private Ordering Through Negotiation.”
    *   **Economic/bargaining theory:** It imports microeconomic concepts (fungibility, present value, discounting, risk preferences) and bargaining theory (including Schelling on credible commitments and “the tactic of decomposition”).
    *   **Socio-legal empirical sensibility:** It relies on socio-legal studies about court practice (“rubber stamp” adjudication; low contest rates) to motivate the model.
*   **How it operates in this paper:**
    *   **Describes divorce as predominantly negotiated, not adjudicated** (private ordering is the modal reality).
    *   **Treats legal doctrine as creating fallback entitlements** (what a court would do if bargaining fails) that become bargaining chips (“endowments”).
    *   **Models negotiated outcomes as functions of five determinants:** (1) preferences; (2) endowments created by legal rules; (3) uncertainty + risk attitudes; (4) transaction costs + ability to bear them; (5) strategic behavior.
    *   **Uses the model to re-evaluate canonical family-law debates** (discretion vs rules in custody; enforceability of promises; lawyers’ roles; courts’ roles in undisputed divorces) by asking: how do these legal choices shift bargaining leverage and settlement patterns?

**KEY ARGUMENTS (3–6)**

**1) Divorce law’s principal effect is on _out-of-court bargaining_, not courtroom outcomes.**

*   **Claim (close to authors’ language):** The paper’s “primary purpose is to develop a framework within which to consider how the rules and procedures used in court for adjudicating disputes affect the bargaining process that occurs between divorcing couples outside the courtroom.”
*   **Logical structure:** Interdisciplinary + explanatory (descriptive-analytic) with implicit normative upshot (reform should target bargaining effects).
*   **Evidence/basis used:** Socio-legal studies and estimates that most divorces are uncontested; comparative references to U.S. and England; citations to Friedman & Percival, Galanter, Hunt, Eekelaar & Clive.

**2) Divorce bargaining is structured by legal _endowments_: entitlements define bargaining chips.**

*   **Claim:** “The outcome that the law will impose if no agreement is reached gives each parent certain bargaining chips—an endowment of sorts.”
*   **Logical structure:** Deductive/analytic (bargaining-under-fallback model).
*   **Evidence/basis used:** Theoretical illustration (stylized example: custody rule allocations; money entitlements), not case-specific adjudication data.

**3) From a bargaining perspective, doctrinal categories blur: “money issues” are largely fungible and linked to custody.**

*   **Claim:** “Marital property, alimony, and child-support issues are all basically problems of money, and the distinctions among them become very blurred”; and “the money and custody issues are inextricably linked.”
*   **Logical structure:** Economic reduction + institutional observation (translation into present values; enforcement/termination/tax differences).
*   **Evidence/basis used:** Microeconomic reasoning (present value, discounting, risk of noncollection), tax-code consequences, joint-consumption analysis, enforcement realities; citations to enforcement literature (e.g., Chambers on nonpayment).

**4) Uncertainty in discretionary standards redistributes bargaining power by privileging risk tolerance and negotiation skill.**

*   **Claim:** Existing standards are “striking for their lack of precision” and thus “provide a bargaining backdrop clouded by uncertainty”; as a result, “the relatively more risk-averse party is comparatively disadvantaged.”
*   **Logical structure:** Analytic (decision theory/risk preferences) + normative implication (ironic/tragic distributional consequence).
*   **Evidence/basis used:** Decision-theory concepts (risk-neutral/averse/preferring); application to custody standards debate (maternal preference vs best interests vs joint-custody rule); uses Goldstein/Freud/Solnit coin-flip discussion as reference point.

**5) Limiting enforceable promises (e.g., Beyond the Best Interests visitation proposal) can _increase_ disputes and destabilize support bargains.**

*   **Claim:** “Limitations on the parties’ power to make legally enforceable promises may, in some cases, make dispute settlement more difficult.”
*   **Logical structure:** Critique via bargaining effects (counterintuitive consequence) + application of Schelling on commitment.
*   **Evidence/basis used:** Close reading of Goldstein/Freud/Solnit proposal; bargaining logic (inability to bind visitation changes custody bargaining and support willingness); Schelling on binding promises; connects to modification doctrine as partial compromise.

**6) Mandatory court processing of undisputed divorces is historically residual, costly, and hard to justify under no-fault.**

*   **Claim:** The no-fault revolution made divorce “largely a matter of private concern,” yet “the shell of the same administrative and regulatory mechanism has been preserved,” imposing significant transaction costs and delay.
*   **Logical structure:** Historical-institutional explanation + normative argument for procedural reform.
*   **Evidence/basis used:** Comparative law (countries using registration); empirical detail (brief uncontested hearings; docket effects); analysis of asserted justifications (ceremony, fairness review, bargaining “audience,” child protection) and why they fail in practice.

**SCHOLARLY POSITIONING**

*   **Agrees with / builds on:**
    *   **Hart & Sacks (Legal Process):** private decision backed by official enforcement as a central institutional feature.
    *   **Eisenberg (private ordering through negotiation):** continuity between negotiation and adjudication; negotiation as rulemaking/dispute settlement.
    *   **Schelling (bargaining/commitment):** enforceable promises and decomposition/piecemeal bargaining as mechanisms supporting agreement.
    *   **Socio-legal “dispute processing” literature:** the courts as routine administration/rubber stamping in family cases.
*   **Challenges (explicitly or implicitly):**
    *   **Court-centered/regulatory accounts of family law** that analyze divorce outcomes primarily through adjudication and treat doctrinal boundaries (property/alimony/support/custody) as practically clear.
    *   **Goldstein, Freud & Solnit’s visitation proposal** by emphasizing its likely _bargaining_ and settlement-pathology consequences.
    *   **Discretion-celebratory custody analysis** by adding a bargaining-power critique: discretionary standards can systematically disadvantage risk-averse parents and raise transaction costs.
*   **Where it sits in debate:** It reframes family-law reform debates (custody standards, court role, lawyer role) by insisting the key unit of analysis is **negotiated settlement behavior under legal backdrops**, not judicial best-practices in litigated cases—making bargaining effects a central metric for evaluating rules.

**METHODOLOGY**

*   **Interdisciplinary analytic method:** a “simple bargaining model” combining (i) doctrinal baseline entitlements (what courts would do), (ii) microeconomic tools (fungibility, present value, discounting, joint consumption), (iii) decision theory (risk preferences under uncertainty), and (iv) bargaining theory (strategic behavior; commitment and enforceable promises).
*   **Socio-legal grounding:** uses empirical studies (U.S./England) mainly to establish institutional facts (low contest rates; rubber-stamp review; brief hearings), not to estimate causal parameters.
*   **Normative evaluation through predicted bargaining effects:** evaluates reforms by their predicted impact on bargaining power, transaction costs, and settlement feasibility, not merely by their moral attractiveness or adjudicative administrability.

**ACKNOWLEDGED GAPS & LIMITATIONS (with direct language where possible)**

*   They deny having a predictive bargaining theory or sufficient data: “no existing theory of bargaining allows confident prediction of how different legal rules and procedures would influence outcomes; nor is there much information about current patterns and outcomes of the bargaining process.”
*   They emphasize incompleteness: “What follows is not a complete theory.”
*   They disclaim definitiveness and highlight lack of theory/data: “Given the absence of powerful theory or systematic data, this essay makes no claims to being definitive.”
*   They explicitly bracket long-run behavioral effects of divorce rules: they do “not attempt to trace out more general, longrun effects” of divorce law on earlier family decisions because effects are “more speculative and remote” and knowledge is limited.

**UNRESOLVED TENSIONS (internal strains, even if unacknowledged)**

1.  **Private ordering vs child-protection doctrine/practice mismatch:** The paper endorses broad parental autonomy “subject only” to neglect/abuse minima, yet also treats courts’ parens patriae role and modification jurisdiction as doctrinally central—even while describing courts as rubber-stamping. The argument leans on _practice_ (rubber stamp) as preferable to doctrine, but doesn’t fully resolve whether the state should _openly_ renounce its best-interests oversight at divorce or instead improve it.
2.  **Indeterminacy critique vs normative commitment to autonomy:** The paper uses indeterminacy (best interests) to justify deference to parents, but indeterminacy can also be read as a reason to demand procedural safeguards (since mistakes are hard to detect). The paper resolves this mostly by privileging parental knowledge/authority, but the tension remains for cases involving coercion or severe bargaining inequality.
3.  **Bargaining model assumes comparability/translation that may fail in custody:** The analysis treats custody as tradable (over some range) and uses economic substitution logic, yet the paper also acknowledges minimum “no trade” thresholds and deep value conflicts; the model’s tractability depends on commensurability that may collapse in high-conflict or principled disputes.
4.  **Court withdrawal from undisputed cases vs safeguarding against unfair bargains:** The paper criticizes perfunctory review and suggests selective scrutiny, but it does not specify operational triggers/metrics for identifying outlier unfairness without recreating administrative burdens or inviting strategic manipulation.

**CONTRIBUTION TO THE FIELD (one paragraph)**

The paper’s core contribution is to _reorient_ family-law and dispute-resolution analysis from adjudication to **settlement dynamics**, supplying a durable conceptual toolkit—“bargaining in the shadow of the law,” legal entitlements as “bargaining chips,” and a five-factor bargaining model (preferences, legal endowments, uncertainty/risk, transaction costs, strategic behavior)—that explains how legal rules function primarily as background constraints shaping private negotiation. It thereby reframes major doctrinal and policy debates (custody standards, enforceability of promises, lawyer roles, and courts’ involvement in undisputed divorces) as questions about how rules redistribute bargaining power, alter transaction costs, and change the feasibility and content of agreements—opening a broader research agenda for theoretical and empirical study of negotiated order across legal domains.

**2\. PAPER METADATA**

**Primary Article (file:3):**
Hazel Genn, 'What Is Civil Justice For? Reform, ADR, and Access to Justice' (2012) 24(1) _Yale Journal of Law & the Humanities_ 397–418​

**Book Chapters (files 1 & 2):**
Hazel Genn, _Judging Civil Justice_ (Cambridge University Press, 2010), Hamlyn Lectures 2008:

*   Chapter 3: 'ADR and Civil Justice: What's Justice Got to Do with It?'​
*   Chapter 4: 'Judges and Civil Justice'​

**Academic tradition/school of thought:** Socio-legal studies; liberal-democratic public law theory; empirical legal studies. Genn occupies the Anglo-American socio-legal tradition, combining normative democratic theory with empirical evaluation methodology.

**Core field:** ADR theory / civil justice theory / interdisciplinary (socio-legal)​

**CENTRAL THESIS**

The civil justice system is a public good that serves foundational democratic and economic functions that private dispute resolution cannot replicate or replace. Genn argues that English civil justice policy since the Woolf reforms has systematically mischaracterised adjudication as a pathology and mediation as its cure, a mischaracterisation driven not by empirical evidence of mediation's superiority but by fiscal pressures and an anti-adjudication discourse adopted by government, parts of the judiciary, and mediation advocates. She argues that while mediation is a legitimate supplement to courts, it cannot supplant the civil justice machinery because (i) the background coercive threat of adjudication is structurally necessary to produce settlement in the first place; (ii) mediated outcomes do not deliver substantive justice — they deliver "just settlement," not "just _settlements_"; and (iii) the erosion of court infrastructure, legal aid, and adjudicative norm-setting constitutes a democratic deficit, particularly for weaker parties.

**THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK**

**Framework name:** Public good theory of civil justice, operationalised through democratic-institutional adjudication theory and empirical evaluation of ADR policy.

**Intellectual lineage:**

*   **Mnookin & Kornhauser's "bargaining in the shadow of the law"** underpins Genn's core structural claim: mediation can only function because courts exist behind it. The background coercive power of adjudication makes mediated settlement possible; without it, weaker parties cannot enforce rights or bring reluctant defendants to the table.​
*   **Owen Fiss, "Against Settlement" (1984):** Genn endorses Fiss's claim that adjudication by judges possessing publicly conferred power serves to "explicate and give force to the values embodied in the law," not merely to terminate private disputes. Genn uses this to challenge ADR promotion as a category error — treating a public institutional function as if it were merely a private service.​
*   **Judith Resnik's courts-as-democratic-institutions:** Genn draws on Resnik's argument that adjudication is "itself a democratic practice — an odd moment in which individuals can oblige others to treat them as equals as they argue — in public — about alleged misbehaviour and wrongdoing."​
*   **Baruch Bush & Folger's four stories of mediation:** Genn uses this taxonomy to map the ideological landscape of mediation advocacy — the 'satisfaction', 'transformation', 'social justice', and 'oppression' stories — before systematically testing each against empirical evidence. This allows her to distinguish between mediation's aspirational theory and its demonstrated practice.​

**How the framework operates:** Genn constructs a two-level argument — normative (adjudication is a democratic public good; mediation is private and lacks this function) and empirical (the evidence from court-based evaluations does not support the claims made for mediation). The normative level is anchored in rule-of-law theory; the empirical level is grounded in her own evaluations of the Central London, Twisting Arms, and other court-annexed schemes. This double structure makes the argument difficult to rebut on either level alone.

**KEY ARGUMENTS**

**1\. Civil justice is an irreducibly public good**

*   **Claim:** "The civil justice system is a public good that serves more than private interests. The civil courts contribute quietly and significantly to social and economic well-being."​
*   **Logical structure:** Normative/institutional — deduces from rule-of-law premises that courts serve democratic functions beyond the parties before them.
*   **Evidence/basis:** Rule of law theory; _Donoghue v Stevenson_ as illustration of how private litigation creates public normative frameworks; Resnik & Curtis on courts as civic institutions.​

**2\. Adjudication creates the normative "shadow" within which all settlement — including mediation — occurs**

*   **Claim:** ADR cannot supplant the machinery of civil justice "precisely because, in civil cases, the background threat of litigation is necessary to bring people to the negotiating table."​
*   **Logical structure:** Structural/relational — mediation is parasitic on adjudication; remove the parent institution and the offspring cannot function.
*   **Evidence/basis:** Mnookin & Kornhauser; Genn's evaluation data showing defendants' refusal to engage absent coercive court pressure; Halsey case analysis.

**3\. Mediation's empirical performance fails to support policy claims made for it**

*   **Claim:** "Large-scale data analysis from court-based mediation schemes compared with control data provides no evidence to suggest any difference in case length durations between mediated and non-mediated cases."​
*   **Logical structure:** Empirical/inductive — marshals evaluation data to falsify the efficiency and access-to-justice claims of mediation advocates.
*   **Evidence/basis:** Genn's own _Central London County Court Pilot Mediation Scheme Evaluation_ (1998); _Twisting Arms_ (MoJ Research Series 1/07, 2007); DCA-commissioned evaluations of Manchester, Reading, Birmingham, Exeter, and Guildford schemes; Ontario mandatory mediation evaluation comparison.

**4\. Mediation produces settlement without substantive justice**

*   **Claim:** "The outcome of mediation, therefore, is not about _just_ settlement it is _just_ about settlement."​
*   **Logical structure:** Normative/analytic — distinguishes outcome (settlement) from value (justice); argues the mediation process structurally excludes legal rights assessment and requires parties to discount claims.
*   **Evidence/basis:** Empirical data showing claimants significantly discount claims in mediated settlements; evidence that mediators use "danger of not settling" as a primary settlement tool; observation that most settlements involve money transfer only, not creative outcomes.

**5\. The demand for mediation is structurally weak when not coerced**

*   **Claim:** "All of the court-based schemes have demonstrated weak 'bottom-up' demand. This is particularly so for cases involving personal injury where the demand has been virtually non-existent."​
*   **Logical structure:** Empirical/inductive — uses uptake data to expose the gap between policy aspiration and litigant preference; challenges the "access to justice" framing of ADR promotion.
*   **Evidence/basis:** Evaluation data across multiple court-annexed schemes; ARM pilot result (80% of referred parties objected); _Halsey v Milton Keynes_ context.

**6\. The judiciary in the lower courts is an under-examined but critical democratic institution**

*   **Claim:** The failure of the academy to scrutinise, describe and explain the work of judges "is an astonishing void in our understanding of the essential functioning of the civil justice system" and "represents a void in understanding of the judiciary as a critical social institution, the legitimacy of which is fundamentally important in a State subject to the rule of law."​
*   **Logical structure:** Normative/empirical — argues that doctrinal scholarship's preoccupation with appellate decisions ignores the social reality of justice at the county court level; frames this as a democratic accountability failure.
*   **Evidence/basis:** Survey of UK and US empirical literature; Darbyshire's forthcoming research; Judicial Studies Board training experience; Moorhead & Cowan "judgecraft" literature.​

**SCHOLARLY POSITIONING**

**Agrees with:**

*   **Owen Fiss ("Against Settlement," 1984):** Genn endorses the core claim that settlement "trivialises the value of lawsuits" and reduces adjudication to the narrow purpose of resolving private disputes; she quotes his argument that judges possess publicly conferred power to "give force to the values embodied in the law."​
*   **Judith Resnik:** Agreement on adjudication as democratic practice and the dangers of managerial judging reducing the visibility and accountability of judicial decision-making.
*   **David Luban:** Endorses his "public realm" argument that adjudication allows "the law to work itself pure."​
*   **Deborah Hensler:** Agrees that empirical challenges to mediation ideology are necessary and legitimate scholarly work.​

**Challenges:**

*   **Lord Woolf / Woolf Reports (1995–96):** Genn argues the diagnosis (adversarial lawyers as the cause of civil justice failure) and the prescription (ADR as systemic solution) were both wrong, and that the reforms were primarily fiscal rather than access-to-justice driven.​
*   **Carrie Menkel-Meadow:** Genn respectfully but firmly contests Menkel-Meadow's postmodernist argument that "truth is illusive, partial, interpretable" and that courts therefore lack institutional authority to adjudicate; Genn treats this as an ideological claim masquerading as epistemology.​
*   **Baruch Bush & Folger (transformative mediation):** Genn tests empirically and finds the transformative story unsupported by evidence from civil/commercial dispute data.​
*   **HM Government / Legal Services Commission:** Challenged for framing ADR as having "at least equal validity to court proceedings" and for conflating cost-saving with access to justice.​
*   **Australian NADRAC:** Challenged for circular reasoning — promoting mandatory mediation on the basis that "the more ADR is used successfully ... the more receptive disputants will be."​

**Position in ongoing scholarly debate:**
Genn sits in the "adjudication romantics" camp (a label used by Resnik for Fiss, Galanter, and Luban) while self-consciously distancing from it. She is not opposed to settlement per se — she accepts that most cases will and should settle — but she insists on the _structural necessity_ of adjudication as the framework within which legitimate settlement occurs. Her position is distinctive because it is not purely normative (unlike Fiss) but grounded in empirical evaluation of ADR schemes. This empirical grounding makes her critique more difficult to dismiss as mere adjudication nostalgia.

**METHODOLOGY**

Genn employs a layered, interdisciplinary methodology:

1.  **Empirical socio-legal:** She draws directly on her own primary empirical evaluations of court-annexed mediation schemes (Central London, Twisting Arms), including quantitative case duration analysis, qualitative litigant interviews, and mediator observation. This is the methodological core of her ADR critique.
2.  **Normative political/legal theory:** She uses rule-of-law theory and democratic-institutional theory (Fiss, Resnik, Luban) to construct the normative framework within which empirical findings are interpreted.
3.  **Policy analysis/genealogy:** She traces how civil justice policy evolved from Woolf through the Access to Justice Act 1999 and Legal Services Commission Funding Code, identifying the fiscal drivers behind the pro-mediation discourse.​
4.  **Comparative:** Brief comparative gestures toward USA, Canada (Ontario mandatory mediation), and Australia (NADRAC), primarily to contextualise English exceptionalism or to import counter-examples.
5.  **Doctrinal (secondary):** She analyses _Halsey v Milton Keynes_ and the CPR costs sanctions as positive law, but doctrinal analysis is instrumental rather than primary.​

The methodology is distinctively **socio-legal** — neither purely empirical nor purely normative, but a synthesis that uses each to buttress the other.

**ACKNOWLEDGED GAPS & LIMITATIONS**

*   Genn acknowledges that her argument is focused on **non-family civil disputes** and explicitly signals its limitations for family justice: "My focus is principally on the promotion of ADR for non-family civil disputes." The family law context may warrant different normative conclusions.​
*   On judicial decision-making, she acknowledges the **scarcity of UK empirical research** as a structural limitation: "This has presented something of a challenge because of the scarcity of UK research on judicial behaviour." The argument for judicial empirical research is therefore more programmatic than demonstrative.​
*   She does not directly address the potential for **ODR (online dispute resolution)** to reconfigure the adjudication/mediation relationship, which is a gap given contemporaneous technological developments.​
*   She concedes that some mediation evaluations do show benefits in specific contexts (particularly commercial disputes avoiding trial) — "time limited mediation can avoid trials in cases not involving personal injury" — without fully theorising when mediation is and is not appropriate.​

**UNRESOLVED TENSIONS**

**1\. The voluntariness paradox:**
Genn insists that "mediation is most appropriate and successful when the parties enter the process voluntarily", yet her empirical argument simultaneously shows that _without_ judicial pressure, uptake is negligible. If voluntariness is the criterion for legitimacy but coercion is the only driver of uptake, the policy implication is paralysis — Genn does not fully resolve how to generate a mediation culture without the coercive pressure she criticises.

**2\. The shadow-of-the-law dependency claim:**
Her structural argument — that mediation depends on adjudication's coercive backdrop — is theoretically powerful but cuts against the scale of its own conclusion. If most cases settle anyway (as she repeatedly acknowledges), then the "shadow" must be functioning even without actual adjudication. This weakens, though does not destroy, the argument that adjudicative capacity must be maintained at full scale to keep the shadow operative.

**3\. Public good without public provision:**
Genn argues that civil courts are a public good akin to health or transport systems, but then concedes "the public courts and judiciary may not be a public service like health or transport systems." This formulation strains under scrutiny: if courts are a public good but not a public service, what follows for public funding obligations? The argument could be pressed further toward a positive state duty to fund access to adjudication.​

**4\. The Donoghue problem:**
Genn raises but does not resolve the question of whether a case like _Donoghue v Stevenson_ could reach the courts today given funding constraints — a genuinely powerful question that, if pursued, would have led to a much stronger positive argument for legal aid. She leaves this as a rhetorical question rather than a sustained normative claim.​

**5\. The judicial under-study argument (Ch. 4):**
Genn argues vigorously that lower court judicial behaviour is an "astonishing void" in academic knowledge, but the chapter itself remains largely programmatic — raising questions rather than answering them. The tension between the urgent normative claim and the absence of her own empirical contribution to filling the void is not addressed.​

**CONTRIBUTION TO THE FIELD**

These works together constitute the most sustained empirically-grounded critique of the English ADR policy consensus to have appeared in the socio-legal literature. Prior to Genn, critiques of mediation promotion — Fiss, Luban, Resnik — were primarily normative and American; they could be, and were, dismissed by English policy-makers as inapplicable to the civil justice context. Genn's contribution is to fuse that normative tradition with a first-person empirical research base: she is simultaneously the evaluator of the schemes she critiques, which gives her argument a credibility that external critics lacked. The phrase "just about settlement" entered academic and practitioner discourse as a corrective to the "just settlement" language of the mediation movement. The Yale article in particular made Genn's argument available to an international comparative audience, opening a debate about whether the public-good theory of civil justice can resist the fiscal pressures driving ADR globally — a debate that remains unresolved, and which has intensified with subsequent legal aid cuts, the Jackson reforms, and the rise of ODR. The Chapters from _Judging Civil Justice_ additionally open, though do not close, the distinct field of empirical judicial studies in England — flagging the democratic accountability deficit created by the academy's neglect of lower court judicial behaviour.

3\. **PAPER METADATA**

*   **Full citation (author, title, journal, year)**
    Marc Galanter, **Reading the Landscape of Disputes: What We Know and Don’t Know (and Think We Know) About Our Allegedly Contentious and Litigious Society**, **31 UCLA L. Rev. 4** (1983–1984).
*   **Academic tradition / school of thought**
    Empirical **law-and-society** / sociolegal studies, with a strong “disputing-process” orientation (disputes as socially constructed; attention to institutional processing, settlement, and “repeat player” dynamics), and a skeptical stance toward crisis narratives (“litigation explosion,” “hyperlexis”).
*   **Core field**
    **Interdisciplinary** (law + sociology/political science; dispute processing; institutional analysis of courts and alternatives).

**CENTRAL THESIS**

Galanter argues that the familiar claim that American legal institutions are overwhelmed by an unprecedented flood of litigation caused by excessive popular litigiousness is largely misconceived: **only a small portion of “troubles and injuries” become disputes, only a small portion of disputes become lawsuits, and most lawsuits end through abandonment, settlement, or routine processing rather than “full-blown adjudication,”** while the observed rise in litigation is not historically unprecedented and cross-national comparisons are weak; the contemporary “litigation explosion” rhetoric (“hyperlexis”) reflects how changes in government, the organization of legal work, and media make litigation a larger _symbolic_ presence even as full adjudication is relatively infrequent, and it functions as a symptom of weaknesses in contemporary legal scholarship. (pp. 5–6)

**THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK**

*   **Framework name (as used/assembled in the paper)**
    **The “Dispute Pyramid” / “Landscape of Disputes”**: a multi-stage, attritional model of how conflicts are _constructed_ and then filtered upward from lived experience into official litigation, with most potential conflicts never reaching courts.
*   **Intellectual lineage / anchors inside the paper**
    *   Builds on the sociolegal move that **“disputes are not discrete events”** but social constructs shaped by perceptions, understandings, and institutions (pp. 11–12).
    *   Explicitly relies on Felstiner, Abel & Sarat’s **“Naming, Blaming, Claiming”** (emergence and transformation of disputes) as a “conceptual map” for the lower layers (p. 12, citing 15 Law & Soc’y Rev. 631 (1980–81)).
    *   Integrates bargaining-in-the-shadow logic by borrowing Mnookin & Kornhauser’s notion that law confers a **“bargaining endowment”** (pp. 32–33, quoting Mnookin & Kornhauser, 88 Yale L.J. 950 (1979)).
    *   Extends to pluralist institutional mapping: courts are one among many “rooms” of justice (explicitly tied to Galanter’s own **Justice in Many Rooms** concept) (p. 34; citing 19 J. Legal Pluralism 1 (1981)).
*   **How it operates in this paper**
    The framework re-describes “litigation levels” as the endpoint of a long selection process. \# METHODOLOGY

\- \*\*Genre-conscious method:\*\* A critical \*book review\* that operates as a \*\*theory-building intervention\*\* rather than mere summary.

\- \*\*Reasoning mode:\*\* Interdisciplinary socio-legal reasoning—combining (i) \*\*conceptual critique\*\* (binary categories; dispute-resolution definitions), (ii) \*\*empirical challenge\*\* (litigiousness claims; complaint-handling and court-processing data), (iii) \*\*comparative/historical illustration\*\* (Norway welfare state; African development courts; colonial/immigrant/utopian communities), and (iv) \*\*political-economy/ideology analysis\*\* (producer–consumer power, state monopoly on control, confidentiality/caveat emptor, microjustice vs macrojustice).

\- Nader emphasizes \*\*epistemic limits\*\* on current claims about American litigiousness: “\*there is presently no way of knowing whether litigiousness is out of control in the United States.\*” (p. 637)

\- She also signals that existing rationales for ADR (court overload) are inadequate and calls for \*\*theories\*\* linking legal change to social order, implying that such theories are not yet fully developed within the disputing literature: “\*Where is the context which might give us a clue as to the theories necessary to explain the phenomena that he isolated?\*” (p. 637–638)

\---

\# UNRESOLVED TENSIONS (analytic pressure points)

1\. \*\*Normative oscillation vs. structural critique:\*\* While urging “prevention” and structural alignment of “economic system” with “judicial ideals,” the paper does not specify institutional pathways for that alignment (legislation? regulation? political mobilization? redesign of markets?), leaving the reform program underdetermined relative to the diagnosis.

2\. \*\*ADR as social control vs. ADR as empowerment:\*\* Nader criticizes state/legal-establishment ADR as siphoning discontent, yet the framework (unilateral/dyadic/triadic) also recognizes that third-party processes can be chosen voluntarily and may be beneficial; the review does not fully articulate criteria for distinguishing emancipatory from disciplining ADR designs.

3\. \*\*“Opting out” as savvy vs. as deprivation:\*\* The paper suggests non-use of law may reflect “sociological savvy” (anticipating “Haves” advantage), but also treats non-voicing as a product of power/ideology; these can coexist, but the relation between agency and structural constraint could be developed further.

4\. \*\*Empirical skepticism vs. confident structural claims:\*\* Nader insists we lack decisive metrics on “too much” litigation, yet offers strong causal narratives about producer power, ideology, and dependency; the evidentiary status of those causal claims (relative to the empirical skepticism) is not fully harmonized.

\# CONTRIBUTION TO THE FIELD

Nader’s review reframes ADR scholarship by attacking the \*\*binary\*\* and \*\*court-overload\*\* narratives that often justify alternatives, insisting instead on a dispute-processing account embedded in \*\*power relations\*\*, \*\*political economy\*\*, and \*\*ideology\*\*, and on a typology of dispute-handling modes (unilateral/dyadic/third-party) that treats “lumping it” and avoidance as central—not residual—phenomena. The paper’s core contribution is to reposition ADR as a \*\*structurally contingent instrument\*\* that can entrench inequality via two-tier justice and depoliticization, and to redirect reform conversation toward \*\*macrojustice and prevention\*\* rather than recurrent institutional oscillations between legality and its “alternatives.”

8….. **PAPER METADATA**

**Full citation:** Hazel Genn, 'What Is Civil Justice For? Reform, ADR, and Access to Justice' (2012) 24(1) _Yale Journal of Law & the Humanities_ 397
**Academic tradition:** Socio-legal studies; liberal-democratic public law theory; empirical legal studies
**Core field:** ADR theory / civil justice theory / interdisciplinary​

**CENTRAL THESIS**

Genn argues that English civil justice policy since the 1999 Woolf reforms has been captured by a fiscally motivated anti-adjudication discourse that systematically misrepresents mediation as an access-to-justice measure when it is in fact a cost-cutting mechanism that diverts citizens away from the substantive rights-enforcement function of the courts. She contends that the civil justice system is a public good, and that the combined effect of ADR promotion, legal aid withdrawal, and resource reduction constitutes a structural democratic deficit: "we are not merely losing the courts and access to them; we are losing the language of justice in relation to a very wide range of issues affecting the lives of citizens" (p. 417). The paper's normative core is that mediation produces settlement without substantive justice — "the outcome of mediation is not about _just_ settlement it is _just_ about settlement" (p. 411) — and that adjudication's coercive backdrop is structurally necessary to the operation of any settlement system, including mediation itself.​

**THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK**

**Framework:** Public good theory of civil justice, operationalised through democratic-institutional adjudication theory and empirical programme evaluation.

**Intellectual lineage:**

*   **Mnookin & Kornhauser, "Bargaining in the Shadow of the Law" (1979):** Genn's structural claim — that "it is only the threat of coercion that brings defendants to the negotiating table" (p. 415 fn.46) — directly invokes the shadow-of-the-law thesis. Mediation is presented as parasitic on adjudicative infrastructure; without the coercive backdrop of courts, weaker parties cannot reach the negotiating table at all.​
*   **Owen Fiss, "Against Settlement" (1984):** Genn endorses Fiss's argument that judicial determination is not merely a private service but the public explication of legal values; ADR "trivialises civil disputes that involve legal rights and entitlements and redefined judicial determination as a failure of the justice system rather than as its heart and essential purpose" (p. 409).​
*   **Judith Resnik & Dennis Curtis, _Representing Justice_ (2007):** Genn draws on Resnik's argument that adjudication is a democratic practice that "brings unwilling litigants to the negotiating table and makes it possible for weaker parties to enforce their rights and to expose wrongdoing" (p. 398 fn.1).​
*   **Lord Neuberger:** Genn quotes his statement that neither ADR nor arbitration "can provide a framework for securing the enforcement of rights and the rule of law" — that without formal adjudication "they would be mere epiphenomena" (p. 416) — as judicial authority for her normative position.​

**How it operates:** The framework works on two levels simultaneously: normative (adjudication is a democratic institution with public functions that private processes cannot replicate) and empirical (the available evidence does not support the efficiency or access-to-justice claims made for mediation). The two levels reinforce each other — even if mediation worked as advertised, it still could not perform adjudication's public function; and in practice, it does not even work as advertised.​

**KEY ARGUMENTS**

**1\. Civil justice is a public good whose value exceeds the resolution of private disputes**

*   **Claim:** "The civil justice system is a public good that serves more than private interests. The civil courts contribute quietly and significantly to social and economic well-being" (p. 397).
*   **Logical structure:** Normative/institutional — deduces from rule-of-law premises that courts perform constitutive democratic functions: communicating civic norms, supporting markets, and checking arbitrary state action.
*   **Evidence/basis:** Rule-of-law theory; _Donoghue v Stevenson_ as illustration of how individual litigation generates public normative frameworks benefiting millions.​

**2\. The 1999 "crisis" was fiscal, not juridical — the reforms were driven by legal aid expenditure, not genuine access failures**

*   **Claim:** "The sense of urgency about a review of the civil courts came less from any new problems in civil justice and more from concern about expenditure on legal aid, and, paradoxically, the rising cost of criminal justice" (p. 399).
*   **Logical structure:** Historical/causal — genealogical analysis reveals that the Woolf narrative of "crisis" was constructed around budget pressures, not evidence of systemic judicial failure. The number of High Court cases was _decreasing_ when the review was launched.
*   **Evidence/basis:** Legal aid expenditure graph 1960–2005; Courts and Legal Services Act 1990; historical analysis of pre-Woolf civil justice statistics.​

**3\. Adjudication's coercive backdrop is structurally necessary for any settlement — including mediation — to function**

*   **Claim:** "The public function of civil justice is explicitly linked with the value of adjudication. Authoritative judicial determination has a critical public function in common-law systems, creating the framework or the 'shadow' in which the settlement of disputes can be achieved" (p. 398).
*   **Logical structure:** Structural/relational — mediation is not an alternative to the legal system but derivative of it; remove courts and the settlement infrastructure collapses.
*   **Evidence/basis:** Resnik & Curtis; ARM pilot data showing that without coercive court threat, 80% of referred parties rejected mediation; Netherlands comparative data on dispute escalation.​

**4\. The empirical record of court-based mediation does not support the policy claims made for it**

*   **Claim:** "Large-scale data analysis from court-based mediation schemes compared with control data provides no evidence to suggest any difference in case length durations between mediated and non-mediated cases" (p. 405).
*   **Logical structure:** Empirical/inductive — marshals evaluation data to falsify the efficiency, cost-saving, and access-to-justice claims of ADR promotion.
*   **Evidence/basis:** _Twisting Arms_ (MoJ 2007); Central London pilot evaluation (1998); Manchester, Reading, Birmingham, Exeter, Guildford DCA evaluations; Ontario mandatory mediation comparison; Dutch Pel analysis. Critically: unsuccessful mediation _increases_ costs by £1,500–2,000 per case.​

**5\. Mediation produces outcome-oriented settlement that discounts legal rights and excludes substantive justice**

*   **Claim:** "The outcome of mediation, therefore, is not about _just_ settlement it is _just_ about settlement" (p. 411).
*   **Logical structure:** Normative/analytic — distinguishes "settlement" from "just settlement"; demonstrates that mediation structurally excludes rights-assessment, requires claimants to discount claims, and delivers "a settlement that the parties 'can live with'" rather than one merited by law.
*   **Evidence/basis:** Evaluation data showing most mediated settlements involve simple money transfer; claimants significantly discount claims; parties' stated preference is for "a fair, inexpensive and rapid _adjudication_ of their claims" (p. 405).​

**6\. Coalition policy has weaponised anti-adjudication rhetoric to effect an "economic cleansing" of the civil courts**

*   **Claim:** The Ministry of Justice "Solving Disputes" paper proposes to "focus more on dispute resolution . . . for the majority of its users, _rather than the loftier ideals of 'justice,'_ that cause many to pursue their cases beyond the point that it is economic for them to do so" (p. 415, quoting MoJ).
*   **Logical structure:** Normative/critical — documents how mediation language has been deployed by the Coalition government to justify legal aid removal, court closures, and compulsory mediation expansion; exposes the circular reasoning: removing legal aid is justified by mediation availability, while mediation is justified by the undesirability of legal aid.
*   **Evidence/basis:** MoJ "Transforming Justice" agenda; _Proposals for the Reform of Legal Aid_ (Nov. 2010); "Justice for All" campaign; Lady Hale and Lord Neuberger judicial speeches; _Justice_ campaign warning of "economic cleansing."​

**Agrees with:** Fiss ("Against Settlement") on adjudication's public expressive function; Resnik & Curtis on courts as democratic institutions; Lord Neuberger's extra-judicial statements on ADR as "epiphenomenal" without adjudicative infrastructure; Machteld Pel on the necessity of dispute diagnosis before mediation referral.​

**Full citation:** David Luban, 'Settlements and the Erosion of the Public Realm' (1995) 83 _Georgetown Law Journal_ 2619
**Academic tradition:** Legal philosophy; civic republican political theory; legal ethics. Luban writes from within the tradition of normative jurisprudence, drawing on political philosophy as primary intellectual resource.
**Core field:** ADR theory / civil justice theory / legal philosophy — interdisciplinary​

**CENTRAL THESIS**

Luban argues that the debate framed by Fiss's "Against Settlement" as "for or against settlement" is a false binary — but not because the pro-settlement camp wins. Rather, the correct question is _against which settlements_, and on _what normative terms_. Luban reconstructs Fiss's implicit philosophical foundations — which he identifies as rooted in a "public-life conception" of political legitimacy derived from Arendt, Hegel, and civic republicanism — and finds them powerful but insufficient to sustain Fiss's wholesale anti-settlement conclusion. He then argues that, under conditions of mass society, expanding caseloads, and monetisation of disputes, adjudication cannot be maximised without producing legal confusion and bad law. The paper concludes that settlements can sometimes realise the public values of adjudication — openness, legal justice, and public-good creation — but only if deliberately crafted toward those ends; and that "secret settlements," which systematically privatise information that carries public health and safety consequences, are the paradigmatic wrong settlement.​

**THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK**

**Framework name:** The "public-life conception" vs. the "problem-solving conception" of political legitimacy — applied to the judicial function.

**Intellectual lineage:**

*   **Hannah Arendt, _The Human Condition_ (1958):** Luban derives the "public-life conception" from Arendt's argument that active participation in political life — the public realm of action and speech — is the highest human good. He extends Arendt (against her own neglect of law) by inserting lawmaking as the necessary counterpoise to the "unbearable unsettledness of politics": "Without law all politics is revolutionary politics."​
*   **Hegel's "objective spirit":** Legal rules and precedents are the visible, objective residue of political action — "the spirit of a political community manifested in a public and objective form." Adjudication gives concrete meaning and expression to this residue.​
*   **Owen Fiss, "Against Settlement" (1984):** Luban treats Fiss as his primary interlocutor, arguing that Fiss's explicit framing (around structural transformation suits) understates the implicit philosophical argument, which operates across all adjudication. Luban extrapolates and restates Fiss's position in a "starker, more polarized form."​
*   **Landes & Posner, "Adjudication as a Private Good" (1978):** Luban uses Landes-Posner's public-goods analysis of precedent-production to construct the _instrumental_ case for adjudication — rules and precedents are public goods systematically underproduced by private settlement markets.​
*   **Immanuel Kant's publicity principle:** Luban deploys Kant's maxim — "all actions relating to the right of other human beings are wrong if their maxim is incompatible with publicity" — as the normative foundation for opposing secret settlements.​

**How it operates:** Luban runs two frameworks simultaneously — problem-solving (Hobbesian/instrumentalist) and public-life (Arendtian/civic republican) — to show that _both_ generate reasons to value adjudication and _both_ generate reasons to tolerate settlement. The paper then argues that "forces of friction" (caseload explosion, monetisation of disputes, legal Babel) make universal adjudication self-defeating even on the public-life conception's own terms. This move converts the question from a binary to a jurisprudence of settlements: which settlements honour, and which erode, the public values adjudication serves?​

**KEY ARGUMENTS**

**1\. Adjudication produces public goods that settlement systematically underproduces**

*   **Claim:** "Precedents and legal rules are public goods" — like lighthouses, they cannot be withheld from free-riding future litigants — and because settlements produce no binding rules, "the private market would systematically underproduce rules and precedents" (p. 2622–2623).
*   **Logical structure:** Economic/instrumental — applies Landes-Posner public goods logic to the settlement/adjudication choice.
*   **Evidence/basis:** Landes & Posner (1978); Coleman & Silver (1986); McMunigal on atrophying advocacy skills; data on declining trial rates (Yeazell).​

**2\. Adjudication is intrinsically valuable as a constitutive dimension of political life**

*   **Claim:** "Adjudication provides occasions for law, and therefore the politics it codifies, to assume tangible form. Take away those occasions and an essential dimension of human experience, political action, will either be extinguished or else degenerate into interminable and unresolved conflict" (p. 2637).
*   **Logical structure:** Normative/philosophical — derives from Arendtian political theory via the formula "contestation, compromise, codification": adjudication is the mechanism by which political compromises crystallised in law receive ongoing reasoned elaboration in light of new social controversies.
*   **Evidence/basis:** Arendt, _The Human Condition_; Hegel; Fiss's account of adjudication as giving "concrete meaning and expression" to values embodied in authoritative legal texts; Finnis on law as "sufficient and exclusionary reason for acting."​

**3\. A world without settlement and a world without adjudication are both unthinkable — the question is "how much settlement?" not "for or against?"**

*   **Claim:** "The locus of disagreement changes from the question 'for or against settlement?' to the question 'how much settlement?'" — rephrased, "Fiss's title should not have been _Against Settlement_ but rather _Against So Much Settlement_" (p. 2641–2642).
*   **Logical structure:** Dialectical/thought-experiment — Luban constructs imaginary legal systems with zero settlement and zero adjudication to show both are pathological; the argument is that the two conceptions (problem-solving and public-life) converge on a middle ground.
*   **Evidence/basis:** Yeazell on discovery-driven settlement increase; modal analysis of hypothetical worlds; structural economic argument about caseload scaling (N² nexuses problem).​

**4\. Under mass society conditions, more adjudication risks producing legal Babel rather than legal clarity**

*   **Claim:** As legal society expands, "adjudication is increasingly likely to confuse the law rather than clarify it" — the N² growth in social interactions generates more potential litigation than the legal system can adjudicate coherently; too many cases make bad law (p. 2642).
*   **Logical structure:** Empirical/structural — applies combinatorial mathematics to caseload dynamics to show that universal adjudication would produce internally contradictory precedents.
*   **Evidence/basis:** Caseload statistics; Yeazell data; Galanter's "litigotiation" concept; Galanter & Cahill (1994).​

**5\. Secret settlements are categorically wrong because they violate the Kantian publicity principle and create public bads**

*   **Claim:** "The sticking point with settlements is not truth but openness. Parties consummate settlements out of public view. The facts on which they are based remain unknown ... Settlements are opaque" (p. 2648); and when secrecy suppresses evidence of public health hazards, settlements "shift the burden to a third party who is not present at the bargaining table."
*   **Logical structure:** Normative/deontological — Kantian publicity principle as test; supplemented by consequentialist argument about public bads (tobacco, asbestos, environmental hazards).
*   **Evidence/basis:** _Cipollone v. Liggett Group_ (sealed tobacco industry documents); _Georgine v. Amchem Products_ (asbestos class action); Texas "sunshine" rule on court records; Judge Sporkin's _Microsoft_ opinion; Koniak on lawyer self-dealing.​

**SCHOLARLY POSITIONING**

**Agrees with:**

*   **Fiss ("Against Settlement"):** Luban endorses the underlying public-life conception and the concern about adjudication's erosion; he is Fissian in spirit though not in detail.​
*   **Galanter & Cahill:** Luban accepts their challenge to the intrinsic desirability of settlement and the "litigotiation" framing.​
*   **McMunigal:** Agrees that declining trial rates atrophy attorney expertise as a public good.​

**Challenges:**

*   **Fiss directly:** Luban argues that Fiss's structural transformation framing has become anachronistic, that class actions are declining to statistical insignificance, and that Fiss's own argument "cannot support the conclusion that the practice of settlement should be dramatically curtailed" in a mass legal society.​
*   **ADR/prosettlement advocates (Menkel-Meadow implicitly):** Luban contests the problem-solving conception as the dominant account of legitimacy, challenging its reduction of the judicial function to efficient dispute resolution. He acknowledges Menkel-Meadow's "important article" but resists her problem-solving frame.​
*   **Landes & Posner:** Uses their framework instrumentally but against them — their public-goods argument supports public courts, not private arbitration.​

**Position in debate:** Luban occupies the unusual position of defending Fiss's conclusions through a more sophisticated philosophical architecture than Fiss himself deployed, while simultaneously conceding that Fiss's anti-settlement _conclusion_ is too strong. He is the most philosophically ambitious voice in the adjudication-defence tradition, but he converts that tradition from a categorical claim into a jurisprudence of evaluation: not "against settlement" but "against the wrong settlements." This makes his position distinctively usable for both critics and defenders of ADR, and explains the article's extraordinary citation record (cited 3,606 times).​

2.  "Fiss's title should not have been _Against Settlement_ but rather _Against So Much Settlement_." — p. 2642​
3.  "A world without adjudication would be a world without public conversation about the strains of commitment that the law imposes." — p. 2640​

**METHODOLOGY**

Luban's method is **normative jurisprudence via political philosophy**, supplemented by empirical legal data deployed instrumentally. He does not conduct original empirical research; instead he mobilises existing socio-legal statistics (Yeazell, Galanter, Administrative Office caseload data) to test whether normative claims are falsified by legal reality. The paper's primary engine is philosophical argument — constructing and stress-testing two ideal-type conceptions of political legitimacy (problem-solving vs. public-life) and applying them to the settlement/adjudication choice. The case-study methodology in Parts III–IV (Georgine, Microsoft) functions as normative illustration rather than inductive proof. The combination is characteristically Luban: legal ethics analysis anchored in political philosophy and illustrated by high-profile litigation.​

**ACKNOWLEDGED GAPS & LIMITATIONS**

Luban explicitly acknowledges that he extrapolates "freely from Fiss's own writing" and that only he is "responsible for whatever stupidities emerge." He concedes that Fiss's structural transformation argument "has not been fully explained" and may be anachronistic given declining class action rates. He acknowledges Menkel-Meadow's valid objection: "legal justice may not be the same as justice. Her point that settlements may be necessary to achieve justice, when justice and legal justice part ways, is a valid objection to the public-life conception" (fn. 86). He does not resolve the "countermajoritarian difficulty" raised by judicial value-elaboration, and his jurisprudence of settlements — criteria for "wrong" settlements — remains programmatic rather than operationalised.​

**UNRESOLVED TENSIONS**

**1\. The Arendtian neglect of inequality:** The public-life conception treats political participation as intrinsically valuable, but it says nothing about access barriers. If adjudication is valuable as public participation, wealthy parties' superior ability to access and shape it is a structural injustice the framework does not address. Luban acknowledges Fiss's "quality of presentation" argument but deflects it rather than resolves it.​

**2\. The monetisation trap:** Luban argues that lawyers transform most disputes into monetary claims, undermining the normative richness the public-life conception values. But if monetisation is structurally ineliminable, the public-life conception's aspirational content largely evaporates in practice, leaving only the instrumental (public-goods) argument standing.​

**3\. The wrong-settlements criterion:** Luban concludes we should be "against the wrong settlements" but the criteria he offers — openness, legal justice, public-good creation — are not clearly operationalised. The paper does not tell us how to identify or police wrong settlements systematically; Georgine and Microsoft are illustrations, not a framework.​

**4\. Publicity vs. confidentiality in commercial practice:** Luban's Kantian publicity argument against secret settlements sits in tension with the entire body of commercial ADR practice, where confidentiality is a core feature parties bargain for. The article applies the principle to public-hazard contexts but does not resolve how far it reaches into ordinary commercial secrecy.​

**CONTRIBUTION TO THE FIELD**

Luban's article performs three contributions simultaneously, each of which proved durable. First, it translates Fiss's implicit political theory into explicit civic republican vocabulary — establishing the Arendt-Hegel genealogy of the adjudication-as-public-good argument that subsequent scholars could engage, extend, or rebut. Second, it converts the debate from a binary (for/against settlement) into a jurisprudence of evaluation, a move that repositioned the entire field: the question is no longer whether settlement or adjudication, but by what criteria settlements are to be assessed. Third, it produced the most sustained normative argument against secret settlements in the academic literature — an argument that has been continuously cited in the subsequent debates over sealing court records, confidential mass-tort settlements, and the transparency obligations of parties who hold publicly significant information. The article's 3,600+ citations reflect its status as the canonical secondary source for the philosophical apparatus underlying both Fiss's position and its critique.

CENTRAL THESIS

Resnik argues that U.S. dispute-resolution has been reconfigured through “Dispute Diffusion” and “Alternative Civil Procedural Rules” such that arbitration—once grounded in contract and consent—has been transformed by Supreme Court doctrine into a mass-produced, nonconsensual regime of court-and-class-action waivers imposed on consumers and employees; the consequence is not “effective vindication” of rights but the “erasure of rights” because few claims are brought, while secrecy and privatized decisionmaking also undermine constitutional and democratic commitments to public adjudication and public oversight of state-empowered judgment, producing an “unconstitutional system” that corrodes both arbitration’s legitimacy and courts’ distinctive constitutional function.

THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

\- Framework name (as used in the paper)

\- “Dispute Diffusion” + “Alternative Civil Procedural Rules” (ACPR) + a public/private account of adjudication and arbitration tied to constitutional legitimacy and democratic public oversight.

\- Intellectual lineage / genealogy (as indicated by the paper’s own framing)

\- Builds on (1) the classic narrative distinguishing adjudication as state public decisionmaking from arbitration as private contractual ordering; (2) the Federal Arbitration Act’s historical linkage of arbitration to “contracts” and “consent” (1925–mid-1980s baseline); (3) “effective vindication” as a doctrinal concept used by courts to justify channeling statutory claims into arbitration; and (4) civil-procedure scholarship on settlement/managerial judging and privatizing court practices (implicitly engaging work like managerial-judge critiques and ADR institutionalization).

\- How it operates in this paper

\- Resnik uses “Dispute Diffusion” to name the macro-shift: courts and arbitration are re-styled as fungible “DR” options, while trial judges are pushed toward conciliation and outsourcing, and arbitration becomes a default imposed by mass-market transactions/employment. ACPR captures the procedural architecture (statutes, rules, regulations, judicial doctrines) that authorizes private binding decisions and confidentiality while failing to secure indigent access, decisional independence, or public participation/visibility. The framework then evaluates legitimacy: (a) whether “consent” remains meaningful, (b) whether rights are effectively vindicated in practice, and (c) whether the public’s constitutional stake in observing state-empowered judgment is violated by closed, private processes.

CONTRIBUTION TO THE FIELD

Resnik’s contribution is to theorize privatization across both arbitration and courts under a single rubric—“Dispute Diffusion” and “ACPR”—and to argue that the contemporary mandatory-arbitration regime is not merely suboptimal policy but constitutionally illegitimate because it simultaneously (1) converts arbitration from consent-based private ordering into mass, imposed waiver of court access, (2) produces “rights erasure” through non-claiming rather than “effective vindication,” and (3) harms the public’s constitutional/democratic entitlement to observe and evaluate binding state-authorized decisionmaking. This reframing ties arbitration doctrine to constitutional structure and democratic accountability, repositioning arbitration’s secrecy and nonuse as central—not peripheral—to the legitimacy debate.
